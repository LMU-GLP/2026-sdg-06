# Los Angeles: Stormwater Capture

## The problem

LA's storm drains were built to move rain *away* from the city fast, because the system was designed against flooding, not drought: "the storm drain systems are designed to quickly direct rainfall and runoff from impervious surfaces into the storm drain system and ultimately the Pacific Ocean. The system was designed to protect life and property from flood impacts." ([LADWP](../sources/la-stormwater-capture.md))

Whatever the capture facilities can't hold "is discharged to the Pacific Ocean via streams, rivers, and storm drains" ([LADWP](../sources/la-stormwater-capture.md)) — i.e., lost as a water source, not because it's undrinkable, but because of how the infrastructure routes it.

## Current capacity

- LADWP's stormwater capture facilities can capture **more than 83,000 acre-feet per year (more than 27 billion gallons)** under average conditions.
- **Centralized spreading grounds alone** (Hansen, Tujunga, Pacoima, and Lopez, which recharge the San Fernando Groundwater Basin) account for **more than 41,000 acre-feet (more than 13.3 billion gallons)** per year of that total.

(Both figures: [LADWP](../sources/la-stormwater-capture.md).)

## What's buildable at neighborhood/household scale

LADWP names these specifically: rain barrels and cisterns, permeable pavers, rain gardens, bioswales, infiltration swales, and infiltration basins beneath street medians and parkways. LADWP also runs a rain barrel rebate through SoCal Water$mart, and a Stormwater Capture Parks Program collecting runoff at nine parks in the East San Fernando Valley ([LADWP](../sources/la-stormwater-capture.md)).

This matters for the challenge: it's a rare water infrastructure gap where a small team could plausibly prototype something at household or block scale, rather than needing to touch regional infrastructure.
