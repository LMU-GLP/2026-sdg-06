# Los Angeles: stormwater capture

## The problem

LA's storm drains were built to move rainwater *away* fast, to prevent flooding — not to keep it. "Historically in Los Angeles, the storm drain systems are designed to quickly direct rainfall and runoff from impervious surfaces into the storm drain system and ultimately the Pacific Ocean" ([LADWP](../sources/la-stormwater-capture.md)). Anything the city can't capture is discharged straight to the ocean.

## The current capacity

- LADWP's stormwater capture facilities can capture **more than 83,000 acre-feet per year (more than 27 billion gallons)** under average conditions.
- Centralized spreading grounds (Hansen, Tujunga, Pacoima, Lopez) alone account for **more than 41,000 acre-feet (more than 13.3 billion gallons)** per year, recharging the San Fernando Groundwater Basin.

(Both figures: [LADWP, stormwater capture](../sources/la-stormwater-capture.md).)

## What's buildable at household/neighborhood scale

LADWP names these specifically: **rain barrels and cisterns, permeable pavers, rain gardens, bioswales, infiltration swales, and infiltration basins** under street medians and parkways. LADWP runs a rain barrel rebate (SoCal Water$mart) and a Stormwater Capture Parks Program collecting runoff at nine East San Fernando Valley parks ([LADWP](../sources/la-stormwater-capture.md)).

## Why this is a strong prototype fit

This is a rare infrastructure problem where the fix list includes things a student team can meaningfully prototype around — a rebate signup flow, a neighborhood rain-garden siting tool, a household capture calculator — rather than something that requires new pipes or treatment plants.

## Open question

No source here yet quantifies what fraction of LA's *total* rainfall or runoff the current 83,000 acre-feet/year represents, or what the unclaimed volume running to the ocean actually is. That gap would need a new source before a pitch could put a "% wasted" figure on stage.
