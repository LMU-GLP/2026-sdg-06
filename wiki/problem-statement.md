# Problem statement

## Region

**South Africa**, with Cape Town's 2017–2020 "Day Zero" crisis as the sharpest evidence of what's at stake nationally.

## Who is affected, and how many

**South African households experiencing worsening municipal water service.** In 2025, **37.6% of households experienced water interruptions lasting more than 2 consecutive days (or 15+ days total over the year)** — up from **24.3% in 2012** ([South Africa water access](../sources/south-africa-water-access.md)). South Africa had approximately **20.1 million households** in 2025 ([South Africa household count](../sources/south-africa-household-count.md)). Applying that 37.6% rate to the total household count gives **roughly 7.6 million households** facing multi-day interruptions — a figure we calculated ourselves by combining these two sources, not one either source states directly.

This is happening even as nominal access barely moves: **87.4% of households had piped water access in 2025, up only 3 percentage points from 84.4% in 2002** ([South Africa water access](../sources/south-africa-water-access.md)). Being "connected" and having reliable water are no longer the same thing. Access has gone backward outright in four provinces — **Limpopo, Mpumalanga, Free State, and Gauteng** — since 2002 ([South Africa water access](../sources/south-africa-water-access.md)).

## The cause: infrastructure that isn't keeping pace

The household-count growth behind that flat percentage is real — municipal water access grew from **9.2 million households in 2004 to 15.9 million in 2025**, a 72.8% increase — but it has been absorbed by population growth rather than translating into a higher access rate or better reliability ([South Africa water access](../sources/south-africa-water-access.md)). Confidence in water safety varies enormously by province, from **92.3% in Limpopo to 64.7% in Northern Cape** ([South Africa water access](../sources/south-africa-water-access.md)), suggesting the strain is uneven and worse in specific places.

## It's not evenly spread

The 37.6% national interruption rate hides sharp provincial variance: **weekly interruptions hit 36.0% of households in Mpumalanga versus 0.3% in Western Cape** — roughly a 120-fold gap — and the share of households with **no interruptions at all** ranges from **74.8% in Western Cape down to 47.0% in Free State and 28.6% in North West** ([Water interruptions by province](../sources/sa-water-interruptions-by-province.md)). Across metros generally, interruptions have risen from **7% of households in 2015 to over 30% now**, and the same 2025 survey names **the City of Johannesburg and the City of Cape Town specifically** as the metros most likely to see prolonged outages ([Water interruptions by province](../sources/sa-water-interruptions-by-province.md)) — which is the direct, sourced link between the national 2025 numbers and Cape Town's Day Zero history, not an assumption.

The four provinces with declining piped-water access since 2002 (Limpopo, Mpumalanga, Free State, Gauteng) together held approximately **29.8 million people** combined per the 2022 census ([South Africa province populations](../sources/sa-province-population-2022-census.md)) — but that is a population figure, not a household figure, and converting it to a household count would require assuming an average household size that no source here states, so we don't make that conversion.

## The proof point: demand-side response works, but only under emergency pressure

Cape Town's 2017–2018 "Day Zero" crisis showed what happens when this strain reaches its limit, and what it takes to pull back from it. At the peak of the drought, city dam storage fell to **21.2–21.4% of capacity**, threatening a shutoff for roughly **4.1 million residents** ([Cape Town Day Zero](../sources/cape-town-day-zero.md)). The city responded by cutting the per-person daily allowance in stages — **100 litres → 87 litres → 50 litres per day** — with the threatened Day Zero scenario itself rationing residents to **25 litres per person per day** from central collection points ([Cape Town Day Zero](../sources/cape-town-day-zero.md)). Consumption fell to **481 million litres per day**, "more than half" of pre-crisis levels, and the shutoff was averted — but only once **rain returned dam levels to 95%** in 2020 ([Cape Town Day Zero](../sources/cape-town-day-zero.md)). The fix that actually held the city together was reducing demand; the fix that actually ended the crisis was luck.

## The statement

**South Africa's roughly 7.6 million households facing multi-day water interruptions in 2025 (37.6% of an estimated 20.1 million total households, up from 24.3% in 2012) are living the slow version of what Cape Town lived fast in 2017–2018: infrastructure and supply that can't be trusted to keep up, in a country where "connected" (87.4% of households, barely up from 84.4% in 2002) increasingly does not mean "reliable."** Cape Town proved that demand-side conservation can buy a city time when supply fails — cutting consumption by more than half — but that response only happened under the threat of an imminent, dramatic shutoff. The open problem is whether households and municipalities facing the slower, quieter version of that same strain can be given the tools and incentive to protect and conserve water *before* a Day Zero moment forces them to, rather than after.
