# South Africa: stalled access and a near-catastrophe

South Africa's national numbers show a country that connected millions of new households to piped water while barely moving the percentage, and where service to already-connected households has been getting worse.

## Key figures

- **87.4% of households had piped/tap water access in 2025, up from 84.4% in 2002** — only a 3 percentage point gain in 23 years, even though the *number* of connected households grew from 9.2 million (2004) to 15.9 million (2025), a 72.8% increase absorbed mostly by population growth ([South Africa water access](../sources/south-africa-water-access.md)).
- **37.6% of households experienced water interruptions of more than 2 consecutive days (or 15+ days a year) in 2025, up from 24.3% in 2012** — service is degrading even for connected households ([South Africa water access](../sources/south-africa-water-access.md)).
- Access **declined outright between 2002 and 2025** in four provinces: **Limpopo, Mpumalanga, Free State, and Gauteng** ([South Africa water access](../sources/south-africa-water-access.md)).
- Confidence in water safety ranges from **92.3% (Limpopo) to 64.7% (Northern Cape)** ([South Africa water access](../sources/south-africa-water-access.md)).

## Cape Town's "Day Zero"

Cape Town came close to a citywide tap shutoff. At the peak of its 2017–2018 drought, dam storage sat at **21.2–21.4% of capacity**, and the city cut the per-person daily allowance in stages — **100 litres → 87 litres → 50 litres per day** — with the threatened "Day Zero" scenario rationing residents to **25 litres per person per day** collected from central points ([Cape Town Day Zero](../sources/cape-town-day-zero.md)).

The city avoided Day Zero by cutting consumption to **481 million litres per day**, "more than half" of pre-crisis levels — a demand-side response — and the crisis only fully broke once **rain returned dam levels to 95%** in 2020 ([Cape Town Day Zero](../sources/cape-town-day-zero.md)).

**Note:** the Day Zero crisis (2017–2020) and the 2025 national household survey are different sources measuring different things — one is a single city's drought emergency, the other is a national, multi-year service-access trend. This page keeps them separate rather than treating Cape Town as representative of the whole country.
