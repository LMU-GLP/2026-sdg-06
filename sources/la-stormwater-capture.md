# Stormwater Capture — Los Angeles Department of Water and Power

- **Source:** LADWP (Los Angeles Department of Water and Power)
- **URL:** https://www.ladwp.com/who-we-are/water-system/sources-supply/stormwater-capture
- **Read on:** 2026-07-30

## Key figures

- LADWP stormwater capture facilities can currently capture **more than 83,000 acre-feet per year (more than 27 billion gallons)** under average conditions.
- Centralized facilities alone (spreading grounds) account for **more than 41,000 acre-feet (more than 13.3 billion gallons)** per year of that capacity.
- Spreading grounds at Hansen, Tujunga, Pacoima, and Lopez recharge the San Fernando Groundwater Basin.

## Direct quotes

> "Stormwater runoff that cannot be contained at these facilities is discharged to the Pacific Ocean via streams, rivers, and storm drains."

> "Historically in Los Angeles, the storm drain systems are designed to quickly direct rainfall and runoff from impervious surfaces into the storm drain system and ultimately the Pacific Ocean. The system was designed to protect life and property from flood impacts."

## The design problem in one sentence

L.A.'s drainage system was built to get rainwater *away* as fast as possible,
because it was designed against flooding, not against drought. Everything LADWP
is now doing is an attempt to reverse that default.

## What can be done at household scale

The page names the small-scale interventions specifically: **rain barrels and
cisterns, permeable pavers, rain gardens, bioswales, infiltration swales, and
infiltration basins beneath street medians and parkways.** LADWP offers a rain
barrel rebate through SoCal Water$mart. There is also a Stormwater Capture Parks
Program collecting runoff at nine parks in the East San Fernando Valley.

## Summary

Los Angeles can capture over 27 billion gallons of stormwater a year, and
everything beyond that capacity runs to the Pacific Ocean because the city's
drains were designed to dump rainwater, not keep it. The fixes LADWP lists are
unusually buildable at neighborhood and household scale — rain barrels, rain
gardens, permeable paving — which makes this a rare infrastructure problem a
student prototype can meaningfully address.
